---
title: "The singularity was near indeed"
date: "2026-10-13"
---
Ray Kurzweil estimated the human brain capability at about 10^16 calculations per second. [1] He then looked at the historical trends of the price-performance of compute. If the exponential growth continued, he estimated that $1,000 of hardware would match the human brain by around 2020. [1]

But of course there is no reason why the technology should stop there. In fact, according to Kurzweil, progress could speed up even beyond exponential. As computing becomes more valuable, more money and effort flow into it, so the growth rate itself rises. [2]

AI that improves AI creates a fast feedback loop which was coined "intelligence explosion" (Good 1965) [3]. Once this intelligence is smarter than its human creators, it escapes our comprehension, hence the concept of the "event horizon" (Vinge) beyond which all bets are off. [4][5]

Ray Kurzweil put the singularity at 2045, when machine intelligence would vastly exceed all human intelligence combined. Runaway self-improvement and progress too fast to forecast. [1][6]

For a long time the only other intelligence known to man was god himself. But if Kurzweil's calculations were right, by 2029 artificial intelligence would match humans [1]. The mere idea of a machine capable of intelligence at the level of humans was, and still is, a heresy. But Kurzweil's argument is so simple that it is hard to attack.

One argument against it is that it really only addresses the power of hardware, but does not talk of software. The singularity movement, as it came to be known, recognized that ever more powerful computation machines would need new types of smarter algorithms to work. Surely better smarter algorithms would have to be invented to make the most of the rapidly increasing capabilities of computers.

Kurzweil wrote that hardware capacity "is necessary but not sufficient", and that without the software of intelligence we would only have "extremely fast calculators" [6][7]. Vinge expected the software to be "a tricky process, involving lots of false starts" [4]. Yudkowsky put understanding ahead of computing power. More hardware lowers the level of understanding needed to build AI, but it does not replace it [8].

Moravec, by contrast, saw hardware as the bottleneck. His 1998 paper argued that AI had stalled for thirty years because research machines were stuck near 1 MIPS, and that performance improves "at the same pace that AI researchers get access to faster hardware" [9]. In his account, enough hardware was what lets researchers write the software.

However software capabilities, unlike the number of transistors on a chip, are hard to measure.  
None of them had a direct measure, so each used a substitute.

Kurzweil measured brain science. Scanning tools were "doubling their overall spatial and temporal resolution each year", brain databases were doubling as fast, and he assumed models of the brain would follow the data [6]. In 2011 he added evidence from algorithms. A benchmark problem ran 43 million times faster in 2003 than in 1988, and he credited a factor of 43,000 to algorithms and a factor of 1,000 to hardware [10].

Moravec measured what each level of hardware had achieved in robot vision, from simple features at 1 MIPS to following roads at 100 MIPS [9]. Vinge gave a date range, 2005 to 2030, without a rate of progress to support it [4]. Yudkowsky said the question has no answer. More hardware lowers the understanding needed, but insight does not arrive on a schedule [8].

Their forecasts clearly focused on the computational power - less on the algorithms. This, as it turns out, was another heretical idea. In today's world we would recognize their position as some early versions of the "bitter lesson" as coined by Sutton. The bitter lesson says that general methods that leverage computation, such as search and learning, trump all other approaches [11]. Moravec came the closest to this, twenty years before the idea had a name. His 1998 paper makes the same argument with the same examples [9].

- He wrote that at 1 MIPS the best results came from hand-crafted programs, and at 100 MIPS learning programs tuned the parameters better than programmers could.
- He expected learning to matter more as computer power grows, and cited character and speech recognition as fields where that had already happened.
- On chess, he credited a handful of good ideas and a ten-million-fold increase in the move sequences searched, and noted that Deep Blue was not designed to think like a human. Sutton's first example is the same case, that Kasparov lost to massive, deep search [11].

Kurzweil agreed on learning and disagreed on design. His 2002 essay says intelligence will not be programmed link by link like an expert system, but also will not simply evolve from a single general algorithm. He wanted a hierarchy of self-organizing systems based largely on the reverse-engineered brain, which is then educated [7]. Building in a brain-derived structure is the kind of human knowledge Sutton warns against.

Vinge set his date largely on the hardware trend and expected working software only once hardware was substantially more powerful than the brain. He imagined the breakthrough surprising researchers who were "just tweaking some parameters". He never argued for a specific AI method. [4]

Yudkowsky seems to disagree. In 2008 he wrote that Moore's law only "lowers the barrier that keeps us from building AI without a deep understanding of cognition" [8]. He did accept the trade-off. A 2001 post says getting AI without knowing what you are doing probably needs far more hardware, for example by brute-forcing an evolutionary algorithm. He treated that as the route to avoid [12].

Perhaps the most convincing critique one can raise is that they got the right results for the wrong reasons. They expected compute performance to continue growing exponentially, and it did, but most of the recent gains were the result of extra spending, not cheaper chips [13][14][15]. Kurzweil expected that reverse engineering the brain would yield artificial intelligence [7], whereas in reality successful approaches used the brain as inspiration at best. They also predicted many futuristic scenarios that remain more science fiction and less reality, such as merging with the machines or having tiny nano robots repair our organs to cure human diseases [17]. However, in my opinion, none of this criticism alters the fact that the singularity group was able to focus on the right trends and use simple extrapolation to formulate a surprisingly accurate prophecy decades ahead of the curve.
